❯ I am trying to understand the limitations of this system. I read that you must have >500 channels for a professional system. Help me understand the context.                 

● This is a useful thing to understand. The "500+ channels" figure is real but needs context to interpret correctly.

  What "channels" actually counts

  The definition of a GNSS channel has inflated over time. Modern receivers count every signal from every frequency from every constellation as a separate channel:

  - GPS alone broadcasts L1 C/A, L1C, L1 P(Y), L2C, L2 P(Y), L5 — that's up to 6 signals per satellite × 32 satellites = 192 channels just for GPS
  - Add GLONASS (L1/L2/L3), Galileo (E1/E5a/E5b/E5/E6), BeiDou (B1I/B1C/B2I/B2a/B2b/B3I), QZSS, NavIC...

  A receiver that can track every signal from every constellation simultaneously needs ~700+ channels. That's where the "500+ = professional" threshold comes from — it means
  the receiver never has to choose between signals and has full redundancy.

  Where your LC29H sits

  The LC29H tracks L1 + L5 only, so it sees at most 2 signals per satellite. With 92 channels and ~30–35 visible sats across all constellations, that's roughly 60–70 channels
  needed — comfortably within budget. But it simply cannot track L2, L2C, E5b, B2b, etc. because it has no L2 hardware.

  ┌───────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┬───────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │    Receiver class     │          Example          │ Channels  │                Frequencies                │
  ├───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┼───────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Professional geodetic │ Trimble R12, Leica GS18 T │ 672 / 555 │ L1+L2+L5 (triple-band) + all GNSS signals │
  ├───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┼───────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Survey-grade          │ Septentrio mosaic-X5      │ 448       │ Triple-band                               │
  ├───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┼───────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ RTK module            │ u-blox ZED-F9P            │ 184       │ L1+L2                                     │
  ├───────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┼───────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Your hardware         │ Quectel LC29H             │ 92        │ L1+L5                                     │
  └───────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┴───────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────┘

  What channels actually buy you

  Channel count is a proxy for three real capabilities:

  1. Triple-frequency (L1+L2+L5): Enables faster ambiguity resolution and better ionospheric modelling. This is the biggest gap between the LC29H and professional receivers —
  not channel count per se, but the missing L2 band.
  2. Signal redundancy: If a satellite is blocked or noisy, a high-channel receiver can still track its other signals. A 92-channel receiver drops the satellite entirely.
  3. Multipath rejection: Professional receivers use multi-signal combinations (e.g., L1/L2/L5 combinations) to algorithmically cancel multipath. Dual-band can do some of this
  but less effectively.

  Practical implication for your project

  Your Float-but-never-Fixed problem (documented in your testing notes) is more likely caused by the patch antenna and limited sky view than the 92-channel limit. The missing
  L2 band is a real factor for convergence time — L1+L5 RTK typically takes longer to resolve integer ambiguities than L1+L2 or triple-band — but the LC29H is capable of RTK
  Fixed in good conditions. Moving to the Beitian BT-760 geodetic antenna is the right next step; that will tell you whether the ceiling is the antenna or the chip.

● The antenna hierarchy for RTK has a few distinct tiers, and what each tier buys you is different:

  What "better" actually means

  1. Multipath rejection
  
  This is the biggest practical factor. Multipath is when signals bounce off the ground, buildings, or nearby surfaces and arrive at your antenna with a phase error. It
  corrupts the carrier phase measurement that RTK depends on.

  - Patch antenna (where you are now): Minimal ground plane, accepts signals from all angles including below the horizon. Worst multipath rejection.
  - Survey antenna like BT-760: Integrated ground plane, better low-angle rejection, designed to suppress some reflected signals.
  - Choke-ring antenna (Trimble Zephyr Geodetic, Leica AR25, Javad RingAnt): Concentric metal rings that destructively cancel signals arriving from below the horizon. True
  multipath rejection. These are what base stations in CORS networks use.

  2. Phase center calibration

  Every antenna has a "phase center" — the electrical point where it receives the signal. It's not perfectly fixed; it moves slightly depending on the elevation angle of the
  incoming satellite (called Phase Center Variation, PCV). For RTK Fixed at cm-level, this variation needs to be either negligible or corrected.

  Professional geodetic antennas are individually calibrated by institutions like NGS (National Geodetic Survey) and the calibration tables are published in the IGS antenna
  file format. The BT-760 is not NGS/IGS calibrated. Without calibration, your absolute phase center position has a few mm of unmodeled error that varies with satellite
  geometry.

  For your rover this matters less — errors partially cancel between base and rover if they use identical antennas. For the base it matters more if you're claiming a precise
  fixed position.

  3. Gain pattern

  Better antennas maintain consistent gain from zenith down to ~5° elevation. Satellites near the horizon contribute important geometry (low-elevation sats improve vertical
  accuracy), but patch antennas have very poor gain below ~15°. More usable low-elevation sats = better PDOP = faster ambiguity resolution.

  The honest answer for your situation

  The BT-760 is the right next step. A choke-ring beyond that would help in environments with strong multipath (on a building rooftop, near a vehicle, under partial tree
  cover) but would not change your Float→Fixed outcome if the underlying issue is the LC29H's RTK engine or the missing GLONASS L2 dual-frequency sats.

  The improvement chain is roughly:

  Patch antenna  →  Survey antenna (BT-760)  →  Choke-ring geodetic
                 ↑                            ↑
        likely gets you Fixed          marginal gain in clean sky;
        in open sky                    decisive in multipath-heavy
                                       environments

  A choke-ring also costs $1,000–$5,000+ and weighs 1–3 kg. For a portable RTK rover that's a different class of instrument entirely.
